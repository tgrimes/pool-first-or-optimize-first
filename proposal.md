## 1. Summary and verdict

The idea proposes a procedure for estimating and reporting an optimal biomarker cutoff when the biomarker has been multiply imputed: average the accuracy curves across the completed datasets and then maximize (pool first), report a confidence band for the whole curve, a set of near-optimal thresholds, and a regret bound in place of an interval for the cutoff, and extend the procedure to a cutoff that depends on a discrete covariate. Its motivation and first result is a closed form for the gap between that procedure's index and the index of the default, which maximizes in each completed dataset and then combines (optimize first). The paper is a constructive method paper (stop 1). The review found no published comparison of pooling orders for a cutoff under imputation and no closed form for the gap: the nearest sources use one order without discussion (Long, Zhang, and Hsu 2011) or settle the same question for propensity scores (Mitra and Reiter 2016; Leyrat et al. 2019). The theory in the idea holds step by step for smooth curves; its quadratic sketch does not cover the empirical step-function curve, whose cutoff is a cube-root estimator (Kim and Pollard 1990), and that case needs its own argument. The NHANES serum cotinine files were opened and fit the application; ADNI and the Pima data were set aside at stop 1. Recommendation: pilot as proposed once the remaining open questions in section 9 are settled, with the pilot's second question (does the gap reach one point of the index at realistic sizes) deciding whether Aim 1 or Aim 2 leads the paper.

## 2. Question and why it matters

**Question (one sentence):** When a biomarker is missing at random and multiply imputed, how should the optimal cutoff, its index, and their uncertainty be estimated, given that the default of maximizing in each completed dataset and averaging overstates the index by an amount set by the between-imputation spread of the cutoffs, and that Rubin's rules do not apply to the cutoff itself?

**Why it matters:** An analyst who optimizes first reports an index that is never below the pool-first index (section 4), so the sensitivity plus specificity attributed to the cutoff is overstated by an amount that does not shrink as the number of imputations grows. An analyst who pools first, as Long, Zhang, and Hsu (2011) did, reports no uncertainty, because no method for it exists. If the answer is that the two orders differ, the reporting of cutoffs from imputed data changes in two ways: the index is read off the pooled curve, and the cutoff is reported with a band for the curve and a set of near-optimal thresholds rather than an interval whose width reflects the flatness of the peak more than the accuracy at stake.

## 4. Mechanism and theory

In words: the mean of the per-dataset maxima is never below the maximum of the mean curve, because each maximum rewards its own curve's luck. Each completed dataset gives its own accuracy curve, and the curves differ because the imputed values differ. The maximum of one curve sits where that curve happened to be high. Averaging the curves first cancels part of that luck before the maximum is taken. The inequality holds in every dataset with no assumption. Its size is set by two things: how far apart the per-dataset cutoffs fall, and how sharply the curve bends at its peak.

The sketch, labeled as a sketch. Write the l-th completed curve near its peak as J_hat_l(c) = M_l - (kappa_l / 2)(c - c_hat_l)^2, with kappa_l the curvature and c_hat_l the per-dataset cutoff. Then the averaged curve is maximized at the curvature-weighted mean c_tilde = sum_l kappa_l c_hat_l / sum_l kappa_l, and

  (1 / L) sum_l max_c J_hat_l(c) - max_c J_bar(c) = (1 / L) sum_l (kappa_l / 2)(c_hat_l - c_tilde)^2.

With equal curvatures the right side is (kappa / 2) times the sample variance of the cutoffs across imputations, which converges to (kappa / 2) B_c as L grows; B_c is the between-imputation variance of the cutoff given the observed data, a property of the imputation and not of L, so the gap shrinks with n and not with L. Carrying the expansion one order further, with tau the third derivative at the peak, the pool-first and optimize-first cutoffs differ by about tau B_c / (2 kappa): they coincide for a symmetric peak and differ in proportion to the asymmetry for a skewed one. Assumptions: a smooth curve with finite positive curvature, every per-dataset cutoff inside the neighbourhood where the expansion holds, and L large enough for the average of squared deviations to be near B_c. A full derivation needs the joint distribution of the completed curves given the observed data under proper imputation.

Where the sketch stops. The empirical curve is a step function and has no curvature. Its cutoff is set by the balance between the quadratic fall of the population curve and the local fluctuations of the empirical process, which is the cube-root regime of Kim and Pollard (1990); Wang, Fang, and Li (2025) state the cube-root limit for a weighted Youden cutoff (their abstract); the Youden case is derived in the supplement, because that paper's full text is not available and the derivation is this paper's own. In that regime the Jensen gap is still positive, but its order is n^(-2/3) with a constant from the limit process, the same order as the empirical maximum's own optimism (Fluss, Faraggi, and Reiser 2005 document that optimism). The sketch applies to the kernel estimator, whose cutoff is asymptotically normal (Yin, Samawi, and Linder 2016), and to the Box-Cox binormal estimator (Bantis, Nakas, and Reiser 2019).

Each result the idea claims, with the first step that is not justified and the check that catches an error.

1. The Jensen gap. Exact for any L: for every c and l, J_hat_l(c) is at most max J_hat_l; average over l, then maximize over c. Equality holds when one threshold maximizes every curve. Check: a unit test on two hand-made curves, and the 0%-missing cell, where every completed dataset is the same and the gap must be exactly zero.
2. The quadratic approximation. The displayed formulas in `idea.md` are correct under the premise (derivative of the averaged quadratic, substitution). First unjustified step: applying it to the empirical curve. Check: the ratio of the observed gap to kappa B_hat_c / 2, with B_hat_c computed from that replicate's c_hat_l, should approach one for the kernel estimator as n grows; for the empirical estimator the gap against n on a log scale should have slope near -2/3.
3. Consistency. Uniform convergence of each completed curve to J, with a unique and well-separated maximizer, gives consistency of the per-dataset argmax (the consistency theorem for M-estimators in van der Vaart 2000), hence of the mean (P2), the median (P3), the mode on a lattice (P4), and of the argmax of the averaged curve (P1). Where the maximizer is not unique, as at the idea's level with two near-equal maxima, the smallest-argmax theorem of Seijo and Sen (2011) is the tool. First step not yet justified: uniform convergence of the imputed distribution function under nearest-neighbour imputation with a fixed number of neighbours, or under predictive mean matching; Long, Zhang, and Hsu (2011) state consistency for the AUC under the usual nearest-neighbour conditions and do not treat the whole curve. A condition that donors exist near every score value where the marker is missing is needed. Check: bias and root mean squared error of c_hat at 100, 250, 1,000, and 4,000 per group, shrinking at about n^(-1/3) for the empirical and n^(-1/2) for the kernel estimator.
4. Non-regularity. Sources now exist for every part: the cube-root limit (Kim and Pollard 1990; Wang, Fang, and Li 2025 for a Youden cutoff; the Youden case derived in the supplement), the bootstrap's inconsistency (Cattaneo, Jansson, and Nagasawa 2020), the normality of the smoothed cutoff (Yin, Samawi, and Linder 2016; Bantis, Nakas, and Reiser 2019). An addition: for the empirical cutoff no closed-form complete-data variance exists, so Rubin's rules on c_hat are not implementable without a smoothed estimator. Check: quantile plots of n^(1/2)(c_hat - c*) and n^(1/3)(c_hat - c*) across replicates in the full-data cell.
5. Discrete markers. With a gap Delta between J at c* and at the best other lattice point, Hoeffding's inequality bounds the probability of a wrong cutoff by a multiple of exp(-n Delta^2 / 2) per lattice point. Holds. Check: P(c_hat = c*) against n on a log scale should be linear.
6. The stacking lemma. Exact for the empirical curve and for a kernel with a common fixed bandwidth, because the stacked distribution function is the average of the per-dataset ones. Not exact for the binormal estimator: by mixture algebra, the stacked group variance is the mean within-dataset variance plus the between-dataset variance of the means, so the stacked fit differs from the average of the fits. `idea.md` should add this case. Check: a unit test that the stacked empirical curve equals the averaged curve to machine precision.
7. The binormal stratum gap of Aim 3. Correct: c_cs(v) is the midpoint of the two means; c_pop(v) solves w_0 f_0 = w_1 f_1, which gives the displayed formula; the two "equivalently" statements (flag when the risk exceeds the stratum prevalence, or the overall prevalence) follow from Bayes' rule. Check: the Aim 3 simulation arm with normal strata against the formula.
8. The conjecture that pooling first reduces the ordinary optimism of a maximum. As L grows the averaged curve converges to the conditional expectation of the completed curve given the observed data, which is smoother than any one completed curve, so its maximum should be less optimistic than a single completed dataset's; whether it is less optimistic than the full-data maximum is not obvious. It stays a conjecture. Check: optimism of the three maxima by n and missing fraction.

Forty-two cells, 21,000 replicates (the 0% cell needs no imputation, so it is one cell per shape, size, and estimator). Each measure is reported with its Monte Carlo standard error:

- bias and root mean squared error of the cutoff under each rule;
- the reported index minus J* under each rule;
- the true J at the chosen cutoff, and the regret;
- the P2 minus P1 difference in the reported index, against its predicted value kappa B_hat_c / 2 from that replicate's cutoffs;
- the difference of the two cutoffs, against tau B_hat_c / (2 kappa);
- coverage of pointwise 95% Rubin's-rules intervals for J(c) at c* and at c* plus and minus 0.5.

Known-answer cells: the 0% cell (all rules identical, gap exactly zero); the sharp cell (c* = 1, J* = 0.6827; the full-data estimators unbiased at n = 1,000 within three standard errors); synthetic quadratic curves (gap equal to the formula to machine precision). Runtime is estimated from operation counts, because no timing exists yet: under a second per replicate for either engine, so about five hours on one core and under two hours on three workers, plus the L = 100 cells. The estimate is about two hours on three cores, to be calibrated with a ten-replicate run before the pilot.

**The three questions and the pass rule, decided before any run:**

1. Does the effect exist? Pass if, in the base cell (sharp peak, 40% missing, 250 per group, empirical estimator, predictive mean matching, L = 20), the mean P2 minus P1 difference in the reported index is positive by more than three Monte Carlo standard errors, and the 0% cell gives a difference of exactly zero in every replicate.
2. Is it large enough to matter at realistic sizes? Pass if the mean difference is at least 0.01 (one point of sensitivity plus specificity) in the base cell for at least one estimator, or at least 0.02 in the 60% cell, or the root mean squared error of the cutoff differs between P1 and P2 by at least 10% in some cell with 250 or more per group. Below 0.005 in every such cell, Aim 1 is a negative result and the weight moves to Aim 2, the close-call outcome. The pass rules are judged on the predictive-mean-matching cells; the nearest-neighbour cells are reported beside them.
3. Is there a fix or a diagnostic? Pass if (a) the absolute bias of P1's reported index against J* is at most half of P2's in the base cell, both beyond three standard errors; (b) across the base cell's replicates, the correlation between B_hat_c and the P2 minus P1 difference is at least 0.5, and for the kernel estimator the ratio of the observed difference to kappa B_hat_c / 2 lies between 0.5 and 2; and (c) the pointwise Rubin's-rules interval for J(c*) from the kernel curves has coverage between 0.92 and 0.98 in the base cell.

### The re-scoped question

**Question (one sentence):** When a biomarker is missing at random and multiply imputed, how should the Youden curve, its maximum, and the optimal cutoff be reported so that the uncertainty of the curve is covered at the nominal rate and the reported index does not overstate the accuracy the chosen cutoff achieves?

**Why it matters:** An analyst who imputes and reports an optimal cutoff now reports an index that overstates the cutoff's accuracy by the optimism of a maximum, about two points of the Youden index at 250 per group in the first pilot, and no interval for anything; no published method gives a band for the curve, a set of near-optimal thresholds, or an optimism-corrected index from imputed data (sections 3 and 5 above). If the procedure works, the analyst reports the pooled curve with a simultaneous band, the set of thresholds the band cannot separate from the best one, and a corrected index.

### Aims, re-ordered

1. **Aim 1 (primary, the first proposal's Aim 2).** Inference for the pooled Youden curve under multiple imputation: a simultaneous 95% band for $J(c)$ over a range of thresholds; the near-optimal set $\{c : U(c) \ge \max_{c'} L(c')\}$, with $U$ and $L$ the band's limits, which contains $c^*$ whenever the band covers $J$ (if $U \ge J \ge L$ everywhere, then $U(c^*) \ge J^* \ge J(c') \ge L(c')$ for every $c'$); and an optimism-corrected index for the chosen cutoff. Two band constructions are compared: Rubin's rules extended to the whole curve (the within-imputation covariance of the empirical curve, which is binomial and known in closed form, plus the between-imputation covariance of the completed curves, with a sup-t critical value simulated from the resulting normal law), and bootstrap-then-impute (Bartlett and Hughes 2020; von Hippel and Bartlett 2021), with two imputations per bootstrap resample and the pointwise variance split into its bootstrap and imputation parts. The optimism correction is a bootstrap estimate of the optimism inside the imputation loop: in each bootstrap resample, impute, pool, maximize, and record how far the resample's maximum exceeds the original pooled curve at the resample's cutoff; subtract the average from the apparent index `[CITE: a source for the bootstrap optimism correction; not in the Zotero library]{.cite}`. Because the empirical cutoff is a cube-root estimator for which the standard bootstrap is inconsistent (Cattaneo, Jansson, and Nagasawa 2020), whether the correction removes the optimism is an open question the re-pilot answers, not an assumption.
2. **Aim 2 (secondary, a closed-form note; the first proposal's Aim 1).** The pooling-order gap $L^{-1}\sum_l (\kappa_l/2)(\hat c_l - \tilde c)^2 \approx \kappa \hat B_c / 2$, its $n$ and $L$ behaviour, and the finding that averaging the per-dataset cutoffs is more accurate than the pooled argmax for the step curve. The first pilot's results are this aim's evidence; the full design adds sizes and shapes.
3. **Aim 3 (secondary, unchanged).** The discrete covariate, with the supplement as fallback.

The paper stays a constructive method paper. The paper test's rows change as follows: row 1, the gap the existing methods leave is now inference (no band, no near-optimal set, no optimism correction from imputed data); row 2, the procedure is Aim 1 above; row 4, the band's coverage under proper imputation and the optimism correction's bias are the properties to show, the gap formula a lemma; rows 3, 5, 6, and 7 stand. Ten data cells (the 0% cells need no imputation, one per shape), 5,000 replicates. Measures, each with its Monte Carlo standard error: the bias of the apparent index (the pooled maximum) against the true accuracy of the chosen cutoff, $J(\hat c)$, and against $J^*$; the same for the corrected index; the simultaneous coverage over the grid of the pointwise Rubin's intervals read as a band, of the Rubin sup-t band, and of the bootstrap-then-impute band, with their mean half-widths; the coverage of $c^*$ by the near-optimal set of each band and the set's mean length; the pointwise coverage at $c^*$; and, for the note, the gap and the root mean squared error ratio of the two orders.

Known answers: the 0% cells, where the bands are complete-data bands and the gap is exactly zero; the closed-form covariance of an empirical distribution function; the sup-t critical value for independent coordinates, $\{\Phi^{-1}((1 + 0.95^{1/K})/2)\}$ for $K$ points, and 1.96 for one; the variance split on synthetic data with known parts; a noiseless curve, whose optimism is zero; the normal imputation's conditional mean and variance under the correct model.

**The three questions and the pass rule, decided before any run:**

1. Does the problem exist? Pass if, in the base cell (sharp, 40% missing, 250 per group, predictive mean matching), the apparent index exceeds the true accuracy of the chosen cutoff by more than three Monte Carlo standard errors, and the pointwise Rubin's intervals read as a band cover $J$ over the grid in less than 0.90 of replicates.
2. Is it large enough to matter? Pass if the apparent index's optimism against $J(\hat c)$ is at least 0.01 (the first proposal's one point, decision D16 of stage 1) in the base cell, or at least 0.01 in the 60% cell.
3. Is there a fix? Pass if (a) in the base cell the corrected index's absolute bias against $J(\hat c)$ is at most half the apparent index's; (b) the bootstrap-then-impute band covers $J$ over the grid in 0.92 to 0.98 of the base cell's replicates for both engines; and (c) its near-optimal set contains $c^*$ in at least 0.92 of the base cell's replicates for both engines. The Rubin sup-t band is reported beside it and decides nothing.

The rules are judged on the base cell; the flat shape and the 60% cells are reported beside it, the 60% predictive-mean-matching cells as the stress case in which the imputations themselves are biased. The status combines the answers as in the first pre-registration: a fail of question 1 or a clear fail of question 3 is a fail; a pass of all three with no deciding estimate within three Monte Carlo standard errors of its threshold is a pass; anything else is a close call stated both ways.

**Runtime**, from the first pilot's timings (about 5 ms per imputation at 250 per group): 420 imputations per replicate, about 2 seconds, so about 45 minutes on three cores for 5,000 replicates; a timing run sets the replicate count against the one-hour box.
