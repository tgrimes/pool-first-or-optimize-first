## Paper type as read

A critique. The procedure in wide use is the standard multiple-imputation analysis pattern: estimate in each completed dataset and combine the estimates (P2 is this pattern applied to the cutoff and the index). The setting it was not built for is a non-regular estimand, the argmax of a step function, whose index is a maximum (so averaging per-dataset maxima is biased upward). Aim 1 states the estimand and sketches the bias; Aim 2 is the correction (question 6 of the paper test); Aim 3 is an extension carried by the application. The alternative reading, a constructive method paper proposing a new inference procedure for the cutoff under imputation, would make Aim 2 the paper and Aim 1 its motivation. The decision is the user's (D1 below records the reading used here).

| Question | Answer, as far as the idea allows | What is missing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A procedure in wide use | Two. (a) The default multiple-imputation pattern: estimate in each completed dataset, then combine (the mean of the estimates, with Rubin's variance). Applied to a cutoff this is P2: maximize the Youden curve in each completed dataset, average the cutoffs and the indices. The idea names `mice` for imputation but no package or default for the pooling of a cutoff. The output is read as "the optimal cutoff" and "its sensitivity plus specificity". (b) Long, Zhang, and Hsu (2011): average sensitivity plus specificity at each threshold across imputations and take the maximum (P1), reported without uncertainty. | Which package and default an analyst would actually use for the cutoff step (`mice::pool` on the cutoff, or `pROC` or `cutpointr` in each dataset by hand), confirmed from the package documentation and not from memory. Evidence on how often applied papers do P2, P1, or complete-case analysis for a cutoff. |
| 2. A setting it was not built for | Rubin's rules combine estimators that are approximately normal with a variance each. The empirical Youden cutoff is the argmax of a step function (the idea says cube-root rate, non-normal limit), and the Youden index is a maximum, so the mean of per-dataset maxima carries an extra upward term from the between-imputation spread of the cutoffs. The heterogeneity that drives the term: the between-imputation spread (missing fraction, strength of the auxiliaries, L), the curvature of the peak, and the discreteness of the marker. | How large the between-imputation spread of the cutoffs is at realistic missing fractions and sample sizes relative to the curvature; the size of P1's own within-dataset optimism (the maximum of a noisy averaged curve), which P1 does not remove. |
| 3. The estimand, as a functional | Stated. With F_d the distribution function of X in group D = d: J(c) = F_0(c) - F_1(c); c* = argmax_c J(c); J* = sup_c [F_0(c) - F_1(c)], the Kolmogorov-Smirnov-type functional of the two group distributions. Aim 3: c_cs(v) = argmax_c [F_0(c given v) - F_1(c given v)] and c_pop(v) = argmax_c [w_0(v) F_0(c given v) - w_1(v) F_1(c given v)], with w_d(v) = P(V = v given D = d). | The uniqueness and separation of the argmax is an assumption, not a fact of every marker (the idea's "two near-equal local maxima" level is where it fails). For a discrete marker, c* lives on the lattice and ties must be defined. |
| 4. The target and the bias term | Sketched by the second-order expansion (the idea's local quadratic). With kappa_l the curvature of the l-th curve at its peak, the mean of the per-dataset maxima exceeds the maximum of the mean curve by about (1/L) sum_l (kappa_l / 2)(c_hat_l - c_tilde)^2, c_tilde the curvature-weighted mean of the cutoffs. With equal curvatures this is about (kappa / 2) B_c, B_c the between-imputation variance of the cutoffs, which does not shrink with L. P1's and P2's cutoffs agree to first order under the quadratic approximation with equal curvatures; they differ when curvatures differ or the peak is not quadratic (step or discrete curves). Both are consistent under uniform convergence and a well-separated maximum. The sketch assumes a smooth peak with finite positive curvature, cutoffs within the quadratic neighbourhood, and a smooth within-dataset estimator; for the empirical curve the quadratic picture holds only at the scale of the cube-root rate. | Step 4 of this stage: a full derivation needs the joint distribution of the L curves given the observed data (proper imputation), the limit of the empirical argmax (the cube-root-rate literature, which the idea does not cite), and the order of P1's own optimism. The sizes at realistic n and missing fraction. |
| 5. A simulation that would confirm it | The idea's base case: binormal marker, n = 250 per group, 40% missing at random through auxiliaries, L = 20, the empirical and the kernel estimator, P1 against P2. Null cell: 0% missing, where every completed dataset is the full data and P1 and P2 coincide exactly (gap exactly 0). Known-answer cell: an equal-variance binormal marker, where c* is the midpoint of the two means and J* = 2 Phi(delta / 2) - 1 with delta the standardized mean difference; and parametric quadratic curves, where the gap equals the formula exactly. | Replicates, Monte Carlo standard errors, and runtime: supplied in step 6. The known-answer value of B_c for a given missing fraction: none in closed form; a large-L, large-n check is the substitute. |
| 6. A correction or a diagnostic | Correction: report the index of the pooled curve at the pooled cutoff (P1) instead of the mean of maxima; a bootstrap optimism correction for what P1 leaves (the maximum of a noisy curve). Diagnostic: the between-imputation spread of the cutoffs and, for a discrete marker, the vote share of the modal cutoff (P4). Reporting correction: the pooled band for J(c), the near-optimal set, and the regret bound in place of a cutoff interval. | Whether the band and the near-optimal set reach nominal coverage under the three variance methods is Aim 2's own question, not a given. |
| 7. The application | NHANES serum cotinine against self-reported smoking, with race/ethnicity as the covariate and plasmode missingness imposed at 20 to 60% so that the full-data cutoff is a benchmark; ADNI CSF markers as the real-missingness example. The one thing it would show: that at realistic missingness the two orders give the same cutoff but the optimize-first index is higher by an amount the sketch predicts, and that the near-optimal set contains the full-data cutoff. | The datasets have not been opened (step 5): the cotinine file layout, its real missingness, and ADNI's access terms. |

No question gets a plain "no": the estimand is a functional (question 3) and the bias term is sketched (question 4). The sketch is not a result; step 4 checks it.

### Verdict

The mechanism is new in this setting. No source found compares pooling orders for a cutoff under multiple imputation, states the between-imputation Jensen term for a pooled maximum, or gives a fix. The order-of-operations phenomenon is known in neighbouring problems (propensity scores: Mitra and Reiter, Leyrat, Granger; prediction: Mertens; variable selection: Wood, White, and Royston), always for regular estimands and never with a closed form for the gap. The closest source is Long, Zhang, and Hsu 2011, which uses P1 for the EPDS cutoff without discussion and without uncertainty. What the idea adds beyond it: the comparison of the four rules, the sketch of the gap as half the curvature times the between-imputation variance of the cutoffs, the observation that the cutoff is a cube-root estimator so Rubin's rules on it lack their premise (with the sources Kim and Pollard 1990, Cattaneo et al. 2020, and Wang et al. 2025 supplying what the idea asserted without a citation), and the reframing of inference around the curve. No prior work of the user's is named in `idea.md`.

Two things the search found that the idea did not have: Zhang et al. 2019's bagged cutoff, which is "optimize then mean" over bootstrap resamples and is claimed to be more precise (the pilot can test whether the same holds for P2 against P1); and Heinze et al. 2013's averaging of distribution functions across imputations, a precedent for pooling a whole function.

## Step 4. Theory check

Started 18:40. Each claim in `idea.md` is restated, checked step by step, the first unjustified step named, and a numerical check proposed. Nothing here is a result; the sketches are sketches.

**Claim 1, the Jensen gap (exact for any L).** Restated: the mean over l of max_c J_hat_l(c) is at least max_c of the mean curve J_bar(c). Check: for every c and every l, J_hat_l(c) <= max_c' J_hat_l(c'); average over l, then maximize the left side over c. Holds with no assumption beyond finite maxima. Equality holds exactly when one threshold maximizes every curve at once. Consequence: the index P2 reports (mean of maxima) is never below the index P1 reports (maximum of the mean), in every dataset, not only in expectation. Numerical check: a unit test on two hand-made curves (exact), and the 0%-missing simulation cell, where all L completed datasets are the same and the gap must be exactly zero.

**Claim 2, the local quadratic approximation.** Restated: if near its peak J_hat_l(c) = M_l - (kappa_l / 2)(c - c_hat_l)^2, then J_bar is maximized at c_tilde = sum_l kappa_l c_hat_l / sum_l kappa_l and the gap equals (1 / L) sum_l (kappa_l / 2)(c_hat_l - c_tilde)^2. Check: the derivative of J_bar is -(1 / L) sum_l kappa_l (c - c_hat_l), zero at c_tilde; substituting c_tilde gives the displayed gap. Both displayed formulas in `idea.md` are correct under the premise. With equal curvatures the gap is (kappa / 2)(L - 1) / L times the sample variance of the c_hat_l, which converges to (kappa / 2) B_c as L grows, with B_c the between-imputation variance of the cutoff given the observed data. So the gap does not shrink with L; it shrinks with n through B_c. First step not justified: applying a quadratic approximation to the empirical curve, which is a step function with no curvature. For the empirical estimator the right picture is J_hat_l(c) = J(c) + e_l(c), with J smooth and e_l a process whose local increments are of order (|c - c*| / n)^(1 / 2); the argmax is set by the balance of the quadratic drift against those increments, which is the cube-root regime of Kim and Pollard (1990). In that regime the Jensen gap is still positive but its size is of order n^(-2 / 3) with a constant from the limit process, not kappa B_c / 2. The quadratic sketch holds for the kernel and the binormal estimators, whose cutoffs are asymptotically normal (Yin, Samawi, and Linder 2016; Bantis, Nakas, and Reiser 2018). Numerical check: (a) a unit test feeding synthetic quadratic curves to the pooling code, where the gap must equal the formula to machine precision; (b) in the simulation, the ratio of the observed P2 - P1 gap to kappa B_hat_c / 2 with B_hat_c computed from the c_hat_l in that replicate, which should approach 1 for the kernel estimator as n grows; (c) for the empirical estimator, the gap against n on a log scale, with slope near -2 / 3.

**A sketch `idea.md` does not have: when the two cutoffs differ.** Carry the expansion one order further (the second-order tool, applied to the cutoff rather than the index): J_hat_l(c) = M_l - (kappa / 2)(c - c_hat_l)^2 + (tau / 6)(c - c_hat_l)^3 with common kappa and tau (tau the third derivative at the peak, which measures the peak's asymmetry). The derivative of J_bar at c is -kappa (c - c_bar) + (tau / 2)(1 / L) sum_l (c - c_hat_l)^2. Setting it to zero near c_bar gives c_P1 - c_P2 = tau B_c / (2 kappa) to first order in B_c. So P1 and P2 give the same cutoff for a symmetric peak and differ, in proportion to the asymmetry times the between-imputation spread, for a skewed one. Assumptions: a smooth curve, cutoffs within the cubic neighbourhood, L large enough that the average of the squared deviations is near B_c. A full derivation would need the joint distribution of the c_hat_l given the observed data under proper imputation. Numerical check: the simulation's flat asymmetric peak (below) against its sharp symmetric one; the difference of the P1 and P2 cutoffs should be near zero in the symmetric cell and near tau B_hat_c / (2 kappa) in the asymmetric one.

**Claim 3, consistency.** Restated: if every J_hat_l converges uniformly to J and c* is a unique, well-separated maximizer, then P1 to P4 are consistent. Check: uniform convergence of J_hat_l to J gives uniform convergence of J_bar (an average of L such curves) and of the per-dataset argmax by the argmax continuous-mapping argument; the mean (P2) and the median (P3) of L consistent cutoffs are consistent for fixed L; on a lattice the modal cutoff (P4) is consistent once P(c_hat_l = c*) exceeds one half. First step not yet justified: the uniform convergence of the imputed empirical distribution function to F_d under nearest-neighbour imputation with a fixed number of neighbours. Long, Zhang, and Hsu (2011) state consistency for the AUC under "the usual regularity conditions underlying K-NN methods" and do not treat the whole curve; a condition is needed that donors exist near every value of the scores where the marker is missing (the positivity-type condition the idea names), plus smoothness of the conditional distribution of X in the scores. For predictive mean matching the same holds with the matching metric in place of the scores. A source for the argmax theorem is needed before the proposal cites it (a textbook; `[TO CONFIRM]` in the proposal). Numerical check: the bias and the root mean squared error of c_hat under each rule at n = 100, 250, 1000, and 4000 per group; the error should shrink at about n^(-1 / 3) for the empirical estimator and n^(-1 / 2) for the kernel estimator.

**Claim 4, non-regularity.** Restated: the empirical cutoff converges at about the cube-root rate to a non-normal limit, so Rubin's rules for c_hat have no justification; the smoothed cutoff is about normal; J(c) at fixed c is regular. Check: the empirical Youden cutoff is the argmax of a difference of two empirical distribution functions, a step criterion of the kind covered by Kim and Pollard (1990); Wang et al. (2025) establish the cube-root limit for the Youden-maximizing cutoff of a marker combination, with the in-sample index asymptotically normal. The standard bootstrap is inconsistent for such estimators (Cattaneo, Jansson, and Nagasawa 2020). Rubin's rules assume the complete-data estimate is approximately normal with a valid complete-data variance (Leyrat et al. 2017 state the premise); for the empirical cutoff neither holds, and no closed-form complete-data variance exists, so "Rubin's rules on c_hat" is not even implementable without a smoothed estimator. For the kernel cutoff the asymptotic normality is in Yin, Samawi, and Linder (2016), and for the Box-Cox binormal cutoff the delta method in Bantis, Nakas, and Reiser (2018). J(c) at fixed c is a difference of two proportions and is regular, so pointwise Rubin's rules apply to it. All correct, now with sources. Numerical check: in the full-data cell, quantile plots of n^(1 / 2)(c_hat - c*) and of n^(1 / 3)(c_hat - c*) across replicates for the empirical and the kernel estimators; the coverage of Rubin's-rules intervals for c_hat built on the kernel estimator's delta variance.

**Claim 5, discrete markers.** Restated: with a unique maximum on a lattice, the chance of picking a wrong cutoff decays fast in n; the vote is natural; P2 leaves the lattice. Check: let Delta be the gap between J at c* and the largest J at any other lattice point. For each wrong point c, J_hat_l(c) - J_hat_l(c*) is a mean of bounded terms (conditional on the observed data, the imputed draws are independent across subjects), so Hoeffding's inequality bounds P(J_hat_l(c) >= J_hat_l(c*)) by exp(-n Delta^2 / 2) up to a constant; a union over the lattice gives decay that is exponential in n Delta^2. Holds. The vote share estimates P(c_hat_l = c* given the observed data) and is a stability diagnostic as the idea says. Numerical check: P(c_hat = c*) against n on a log scale should be linear in the discrete cell.

**The stacking lemma.** Restated: stacking the L completed datasets and running one empirical or fixed-bandwidth kernel analysis gives J_bar exactly, because D is observed and every completed dataset has the same group sizes. Check: the stacked empirical distribution function in group d is (1 / (L n_d)) sum_l sum_i 1{X_il <= c} = (1 / L) sum_l F_hat_dl(c). Exact. For a kernel estimator with a common fixed bandwidth the same average holds. Not exact for the Box-Cox binormal estimator: by mixture algebra, the stacked group variance equals the mean of the within-dataset variances plus the between-dataset variance of the means, so stacking inflates the fitted variances relative to the average of the per-dataset fits, and the stacked cutoff is not P1's. `idea.md` says "unless the bandwidth is re-chosen on the stacked data" and should add the binormal case. Numerical check: a unit test that the stacked empirical curve equals J_bar to machine precision; the binormal case shown to differ.

**The binormal stratum gap (Aim 3).** Restated: with X given D = d, V = v normal with mean mu_d(v) and common variance sigma^2, c_pop(v) - c_cs(v) = sigma^2 log{w_0(v) / w_1(v)} / (mu_1(v) - mu_0(v)). Check: c_cs(v) solves f_0 = f_1, which gives the midpoint of the means. c_pop(v) solves w_0 f_0 = w_1 f_1; taking logs, (c - mu_1)^2 - (c - mu_0)^2 = -2 sigma^2 log(w_0 / w_1), and expanding gives c_pop(v) = (mu_0 + mu_1) / 2 + sigma^2 log(w_0 / w_1) / (mu_1 - mu_0). Correct. The two "equivalently" statements also hold: the stratum rule flags a subject when f_1(x given v) / f_0(x given v) > 1, which is P(D = 1 given x, v) above the stratum prevalence; the population rule flags when w_1 f_1 > w_0 f_0, which is P(D = 1 given x, v) above the overall prevalence, by Bayes' rule. The weights w_d(v) = P(V = v given D = d) involve only observed variables, as the idea says. Numerical check: the simulation's Aim 3 arm with normal strata, where the two cutoffs are computed from the fitted model and compared with the formula.

**The conjecture (P1 reduces the maximum-of-a-noisy-curve optimism).** Restated: averaging over imputations smooths the imputed part of the noise before the maximum is taken. Assessment: as L grows, J_bar converges to the conditional expectation of J_hat given the observed data, in which each imputed subject contributes its conditional distribution function rather than a step, so J_bar is smoother than any single completed curve and its maximum should be less optimistic than a single completed dataset's. Whether it is less optimistic than the full-data curve is not obvious, because the imputed subjects carry less information than observed ones. The conjecture is testable and stays a conjecture. Numerical check: optimism of max J_bar against optimism of max J_hat_l and of the full-data maximum, by n and missing fraction.

The non-regularity claims had no citation in `idea.md`; the search supplied Kim and Pollard (1990), Cattaneo, Jansson, and Nagasawa (2020), Wang et al. (2025), and Yin, Samawi, and Linder (2016).

1. Does the effect exist? Pass if, in the base cell (sharp peak, 40% missing, n = 250, empirical estimator, L = 20), the mean P2 - P1 difference in the reported index is positive by more than three Monte Carlo standard errors, and the 0% cell gives a difference of exactly zero in every replicate.
2. Is it large enough to matter at realistic sizes? 3. Is there a fix or a diagnostic?
